How modes work

01

A center, not a starting note

C major and D Dorian share the same notes. But in D Dorian, D feels like home. Beginning a C-major scale on D doesn’t create that feeling by itself; melody, bass, and harmony establish the center.

02

Compare from the same home

To hear each mode’s identity clearly, keep the tonic fixed. C Ionian has E and B; C Dorian has E♭ and B♭. These are parallel modes: the same center, different interval recipes.

Try it above: turn on the tonic drone, then switch modes.

03

Establish the mode with harmony

A short repeating backdrop, called a vamp, lets a mode settle in. Favor its characteristic tone and return to the tonic. A strong major-key cadence can pull your ear into a different tonal center.

Modes are musical ingredients—not fixed moods, genres, or finger patterns.

How the seven modes relate to a major scale

These are relative modes of C major. Each uses the white keys, but each gives a different note the role of tonic. The explorer above instead compares parallel modes.

What do ♭, ♯, and the interval numbers mean?

Numbers label scale degrees measured from the tonic: 1 is home, 3 is the third degree, and so on. ♭ lowers a degree by one semitone; ♯ raises it. The recipes here are compared with the major scale on the same tonic. A whole step (W) is two semitones; a half step (H) is one.

Is Locrian really a usable mode?

Yes, but it is unusually unstable: its fifth is diminished, so its tonic triad is diminished too. A sustained tonic bass and a sparse melody help make its center audible. Treat its instability as a color to explore, not a mistake to fix.
